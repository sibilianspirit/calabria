Piazza San Rocco in picturesque Scilla is an essential stop for anyone visiting Calabria. Situated above the Strait of Messina, the square offers views that truly impress. It is here that you can admire the panorama of the Calabrian and Sicilian coastlines, and in favourable conditions even the distant outline of Stromboli. From the square you can clearly see how the town is arranged around three distinct areas: upper San Giorgio, beachside Marina Grande and fishing Chianalea. Piazza San Rocco serves as an important point of social life in the upper part of Scilla, situated approximately 70 metres above sea level.

Piazza San Rocco lies in the upper part of Scilla, above Marina Grande and Chianalea. Walking up from the beach area or railway station typically takes around 10–20 minutes, depending on the route and fitness, and the path leads uphill through steps and lanes of the old town. The square is freely accessible and is best visited at sunset or after dark, when the lights of the Sicilian coast appear across the strait.

History of Piazza San Rocco – From Myths to Modernity

Piazza San Rocco in Scilla, though currently known primarily as one of the most important viewpoints over the Strait of Messina, conceals far more than just charming landscapes. The history of this place is closely linked to the cultural and historical heritage of Calabria and Scilla's position above one of the most important maritime passages in southern Italy. The square is best understood as part of the upper fabric of the town, from which people have looked out over the strait and the movement on the water for centuries.

Scylla appears in Greek mythology and Homer's Odyssey as a sea monster associated with the narrow passage opposite Charybdis. Scilla sits by the Strait of Messina, at a site considered strategic for navigation between the Tyrrhenian and Ionian Seas since antiquity. Ancient authors wrote about the defensive significance of Scilla's rock, and later phases of fortification were associated with the Byzantine, Norman and early modern periods.

Medieval Roots and Urban Evolution

Scilla, whose name evokes the mythological tales of the monster Scylla, has long been a place of great strategic importance, controlling the shipping route between Italy and Sicily. The square's present appearance is the result of years of transformation and reconstruction following the devastating earthquakes of 1783 and 1908, which left a clear mark on the entire town. Today's space therefore combines the daily life of residents with the memory of successive stages in Scilla's development.

Architectural Relics and Seismic Resilience

Although precise documents describing the early development of Piazza San Rocco are lacking, it is easy to see how Scilla's past continues to influence this space to this day. The stone streets leading to the square recall that the upper part of the town was linked both to the daily life of residents and to observing the strait. The square's strategic position enabled monitoring of ship traffic, which for centuries was of defensive significance.

Contemporary Significance and Revitalisation

Today, Scilla attracts visitors with a combination of natural beauty and history stretching from mythology to modern times. Piazza San Rocco remains above all an important viewpoint and meeting place, where residents and visitors pause to look out over the strait, the castle, and the Sicilian lights on the other side of the water. It is one of those places that best shows the character of Scilla – a town suspended between rock, sea and legend.

Church of San Rocco – The Square's Spiritual Heart

The dominant feature of the square is the Church of San Rocco, dedicated to Saint Roch, one of the most important saints of the local cult. Its facade gives the whole square a clear focal point and ensures the space has not only a scenic but also a religious and social character. The oldest documents confirming the existence of the Church of San Rocco in Scilla come from the pastoral visit of Archbishop Annibale D’Afflitto in 1594.

The new church was built between 1738 and 1751. The earthquake of 28 December 1908 destroyed the church facade and roof, and it reopened for worship on 16 August 1990. The main altar contains a marble statue of San Rocco from the first half of the 16th century, attributed to Giovan Battista Mazzolo. It is here that one can most easily see how strongly Scilla's daily life is intertwined with local tradition.

The feast of San Rocco in Scilla begins around 16 August, the saint's liturgical feast day, while the main local celebrations with processions and the Trionfino usually take place on the weekend after 16 August; check the programme for the specific year before travelling. The celebrations include a mass, a procession carrying the saint's statue through the town, and evening events on the square. Local tradition includes processions through the districts of Chianalea, Marina Grande and San Giorgio.

The culminating moment of the procession is the traditional “trionfino” on Piazza San Rocco. The saint's statue is then carried at a run by the portatori beneath a cascade of fireworks. It is the most recognisable element of the feast and one of the moments when the square becomes the centre of life for all of Scilla.

The Strait of Messina – Key Hub of Mediterranean Navigation

The Strait of Messina is a narrow sea passage that plays a vital role in the geography of the central Mediterranean. It is about 33 km long, and its width ranges from roughly 3.1 km to 16 km. The narrowest stretch lies in the north, between Capo Peloro on the Sicilian side and the Torre Cavallo area and Villa San Giovanni coast on the Calabrian side. It is from here, from Piazza San Rocco, that one can best see how close Calabria and Sicily lie to each other.

Geographically, the strait connects the Tyrrhenian Sea with the Ionian Sea and separates Sicily from Calabria. It creates a unique area of strong sea currents, well known to sailors and fishermen. The strategic importance of the Strait of Messina is hard to overstate, as for centuries it has controlled movement between different parts of the Mediterranean. For visitors to Scilla, however, it is above all a landscape that changes with the weather and the light.

In good visibility one can see the Sicilian coast from here, and sometimes even the distant outline of Etna. After dark the panorama changes character: instead of the coastline, the lights of Messina, Capo Peloro and towns on both sides of the strait become most visible. From Piazza San Rocco it is also easy to understand why this short stretch of sea has so strongly shaped the history of Scilla.

Ruffo Castle – Guardian of History and Cultural Heart of Scilla

Ruffo Castle in Scilla, also known as Castello Ruffo di Scilla, is one of the most important viewpoints over the Strait of Messina. Perched on a lofty rock, the castle enables visitors to admire the landscapes of Costa Viola and the picturesque Chianalea district. The fortifications on Scilla's rock have ancient roots, and the present castle complex was rebuilt in the Middle Ages and the early modern period.

The castle has been associated with Byzantine and Norman phases, and later with the Ruffo di Calabria family. In 1533 the fortifications were expanded during the period of the Spanish viceroyalty in response to Ottoman and corsair threats, and the site continued to serve military functions into the modern era. Today the castle is an important historical and scenic point, which pairs naturally with a walk around Piazza San Rocco and the upper part of Scilla.

According to the Comune di Scilla website, the castle is accessible from Piazza Matrice. The visiting hours given by the municipality are: Monday closed, Tuesday–Sunday 09:00–13:40 and 15:00–20:00. A standard ticket costs €3.00, while children up to 6 years old and people with certified disabilities with a companion enter free of charge. The Aeolian Islands Seen from Piazza San Rocco – Calabria's Panorama

Piazza San Rocco offers a wide view over the Strait of Messina and the coastlines of Calabria and Sicily. From the square you can usually see the Sicilian coast around Messina and Capo Peloro (Punta del Faro), as well as Ruffo Castle, Marina Grande and Chianalea. Stromboli can be visible when the air is exceptionally clear, especially after rain or in dry conditions, while the other Aeolian Islands are not constantly or easily visible.

From the terrace, one can admire the imposing cliffs of Costa Viola, which often take on violet-pink tones at sunset. The name Costa Viola refers precisely to these colours of the sea and rocks, seen especially in the evening along the stretch of coast between Villa San Giovanni, Scilla, Bagnara Calabra and Palmi. The area is also known for traditional swordfish fishing in the Strait of Messina using felucche and tall observation masts.

Frequently Asked Questions

What can you see from Piazza San Rocco in Scilla?

The square offers a panoramic view of the Strait of Messina, the Sicilian coastline around Messina and Capo Peloro, Ruffo Castle, Marina Grande and Chianalea. When the air is exceptionally clear, Stromboli can be visible, while Etna appears only in rare conditions.

Where is the best viewpoint in Scilla?

Piazza San Rocco is considered the best publicly accessible viewpoint, alongside the terraces of Ruffo Castle. The square offers a wide perspective over the entire area from a height of approximately 70 metres.

Can you see Sicily from Piazza San Rocco?

Yes, Sicily is usually very clearly visible from the square, especially the stretch of coast around Messina and Capo Peloro. After dark, the lights on the other side of the strait are also easy to see.

How do you get to Piazza San Rocco from the beach?

The simplest way is to walk from the Marina Grande area or the railway station. The climb usually takes around 10–20 minutes and leads uphill through steps and lanes of the old town.

When does the San Rocco festival take place in Scilla?

The feast of San Rocco begins around 16 August, while the main local celebrations with processions and the Trionfino usually take place on the weekend after 16 August. The programme can vary from year to year, so check the current dates before travelling.
