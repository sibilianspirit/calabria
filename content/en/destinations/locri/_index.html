Locri, known in antiquity as Lokroi Epizephyrioi, was one of the great pillars of Magna Graecia civilisation. This Greek colony, founded in the 7th century BC by Greeks from Locris, mainly Opuntian and Ozolian Locris, developed on the Ionian coast of Calabria and remains one of the region’s most important archaeological sites. Its position on the Ionian Sea, with access to the inland hills and the Serre and Aspromonte massifs, gave the city commercial and political importance. Modern Locri lets you combine the ruins, the museum and the beaches of the Costa dei Gelsomini without separating history from the real landscape of this part of Calabria.

The Foundation of Locri – Foundations of Greek Culture in Italy

The Origins of Locri – Myth and History

Locri Epizephirii, one of the oldest Greek colonies in Magna Graecia, is an example of the inseparable intertwining of foundation traditions and historical fact. The founding of Locri is dated to around 673–660 BC, a period of intense Greek cultural expansion in the south of the Italian Peninsula. According to one tradition, the expedition’s oikist was Evantes; other accounts link the city’s origins with women from noble families and their slaves, who arrived while their husbands were away fighting in the Messenian War.

There is, however, a second – more prosaic yet equally fascinating – account of the city’s origins, transmitted by Aristotle. According to this version, Locri was founded by a group of servants and lower-class individuals who fled Greece with their mistresses while their husbands were occupied with war. This duality of origins – aristocratic versus plebeian – shaped the unique social identity of a city that at its peak may have numbered as many as 25,000 inhabitants.

Greek Roots and Strategic Choices

Locri was settled by colonists from Opuntian and Ozolian Locris; Treccani also allows for the presence of elements from Phocis and from Corinthian and Ionian circles. The settlers chose the Ionian coastline for its strategic position between the Gulf of Squillace and the Strait of Messina. The name “Epizephirii” refers directly to this location – it means “beyond the west wind” (Zephyr), distinguishing the new settlement from its ancestral Locris in Greece. This placement guaranteed access to the sea and to the inland hills and the Serre and Aspromonte massifs.

Locri’s Influence on Magna Graecia

Locri rapidly became one of the main political and cultural centres of the region. Its significance is underlined by the introduction of the unique legal system devised by Zaleucus, who created one of the first written law codes in the western world. This code, known for its severity and pursuit of absolute social order, introduced principles including the law of retaliation (an eye for an eye) and other regulations that later influenced law in other colonies such as Croton and Catana. It was here, around 660 BC, that the concept was born that law should be fixed and known to all – not dependent on the whim of a judge.

Culture and Religion – the Legacy of Locri

Locri also became renowned for its flourishing cult of Aphrodite Epizephyris, centred on one of the city’s important sanctuaries. The Temple of Aphrodite in Locri was an important place of worship in Magna Graecia, showing the role of religion in the social life of this polis. Nor can one overlook the figure of Nossis, who lived at the turn of the 4th and 3rd centuries BC and was called the “Sappho of Italy”. Her poetry, often dedicated to women and Aphrodite herself, is a rare example of a female voice in ancient literature, proving that Locri was a centre not only of law but also of Hellenistic art.

Economy and Trade – the Strength of Locri

Thanks to its position on the Ionian coast and the fertile soils of Calabria, Locri developed as a significant trading centre. The production of olives, wine and grain formed the basis of the local economy, and the monetary system with its distinctive designs emphasised the colony’s independence and economic self-sufficiency. These factors made Locri a strategic point on the regional trade map, exchanging goods with neighbouring civilisations and peoples across the Mediterranean basin.

Locri on the Ionian Sea – Key Role in Greek Colonial Strategy

Locri’s Strategic Geographic Assets

Locri’s position on the Ionian Sea was of great strategic importance in the context of the Greek presence in southern Italy. The city benefited from access to the sea, contacts with Sicily and Greece, and an agricultural hinterland that made it possible to sustain a strong polis.

Defensive Position and Natural Terrain

Ancient Locri extended from the low coastline and alluvial plain towards gentle hills; its defence relied above all on an extensive system of city walls, not on cliffs. This location gave the city both access to the sea and the ability to control the terrain where the coast met the interior of the Locride. In conflicts with neighbouring cities, natural conditions mattered, but so did defensive infrastructure and alliances.

Control of Trade Routes

Locri’s strategic position enabled control over sea routes, particularly thanks to proximity to the important trading centres of Sicily and Greece. Archaeological finds – ceramics and coins – point to intensive trade with other colonies, forming the basis of the city’s economic development. Locri was a central hub for the exchange of goods such as olive oil, wine and grain, which increased its wealth and political importance in the region. The location of ancient Locri’s port remains uncertain; researchers suggest it may have functioned as a simple coastal landing place or as a harbour at the mouth of one of the fiumare.

Involvement in Regional Conflicts

Locri played an important role in numerous conflicts fought in this part of the ancient world. Over the centuries the city rivalled its neighbours, maintaining its influence through maritime and political alliances. During conflicts with Croton, Reggio and other cities of southern Italy, Locri relied on alliances, especially with Syracuse. Its alliance with Dionysius of Syracuse strengthened the city’s defensive and offensive capabilities, making Locri one of the significant Greek colonies in southern Italy.

Influence on Regional Development

Locri was not only militarily but also economically and culturally significant within Magna Graecia. Its influence was felt throughout the region, and numerous archaeological finds continue to provide information about its rich civilisational heritage. Although modern architecture and urban development have transformed the landscape, the remains of this ancient centre still stand and continue to attract both archaeologists and visitors interested in ancient heritage, in the form of the ruins of Locri Epizephyrii.

The Code of Zaleucus – Foundation of Ancient Greek Jurisprudence

The Code of Zaleucus – Innovation and Historical Significance

Zaleucus, one of the first known lawmakers, was active in the 7th century BC in Locri, an important Greek colony in Calabria. His work on the codification of law was pioneering in the Greek world of the time and had a profound influence on the subsequent development of European legal systems. Active around 660 BC, he anticipated later Athenian reforms, becoming the first Western lawmaker to understand the importance of the written word in legal proceedings.

At a time when Greek city-states known as “poleis” were beginning to systematise their internal regulations, Zaleucus was introducing groundbreaking changes in legislation. Lokroi Epizefyri, the colony he led, needed clear and unified principles, and so Zaleucus drew up one of the first known written legal codes. His code not only regulated everyday life in the city but also served as a model for other Greek lawmakers.

Key Features of Zaleucus’s Legislation

The Code of Zaleucus was distinguished by several key characteristics:

  • Systematisation and structure – Zaleucus sought to create a comprehensive legal system covering all aspects of social and family life. Criminal, property and family law were organised in a uniform manner, facilitating interpretation and application.
  • Equality before the law – One of the main principles of the code was that all inhabitants were subject to the same laws regardless of their social standing – a significant step forward compared to the earlier tradition of customary law.
  • Severity of penalties – The Code of Zaleucus was known for its rigorous punishments, aimed at ensuring order and respect for the law in the colony. Although some penalties were harsh, they served a preventive function in colonial society.

Legacy of Zaleucus

The influence of Zaleucus on the later development of Greek and European legislation is significant. While his code set a precedent for other Greek cities, such as Athens, where Draco and Solon later introduced their reforms, it established the foundations for the transition from oral to written law. Legends say that Zaleucus was so devoted to the rule of law that when his own son broke a law punishable by blinding, the lawmaker ordered one of his son’s eyes and one of his own to be put out, to satisfy the letter of the law while preserving his heir’s sight.

Although many details of Zaleucus’s life and work are shrouded in legend, his contribution to the development of legal systems remains one of the most important elements of Locri’s tradition. Tradition attributes so-called divine inspiration to him, and some stories suggest that he preserved the impartiality of the law despite personal cost. The bronze tablets from the archive of the sanctuary of Olympian Zeus, dated mainly to the 4th–3rd centuries BC, document the financial operations of the polis and the economic role of the sanctuary. They are not the preserved code of Zaleucus, but they show that Locri had developed administrative and economic mechanisms.

The Cult of Aphrodite in Locri – from the Heart of Religion to the City’s Identity

At the heart of Locri Epizefiroi, the cult of Aphrodite played a significant role that extended beyond traditional religious boundaries, influencing the social, economic and cultural life of the city. Aphrodite, goddess of love and fertility, was not merely an object of veneration but a symbol that integrated diverse aspects of urban life. Alongside her, the cult of Persephone was extremely strong, as confirmed by finds from the Mannella sanctuary.

Religious and Social Pathways

The Temple of Aphrodite in Locri was one of the pillars of her cult. Drawing worshippers from different corners of Magna Graecia, it formed the foundation of the city’s religious life and a point of reference for its identity. Celebrations associated with the cult, such as processions and festivals, were part of the civic calendar, attracting pilgrims and involving the inhabitants of Locri. Women frequently played essential roles as priestesses and participants in rituals – which corresponds with the poetry of Nossis, who created her works in precisely this religious context.

Economic Impact and Artistic Inspiration

The significant influx of pilgrims visiting the Temple of Aphrodite supported the local economy. Revenue generated by the cult – in the form of offerings and gifts – contributed to the city’s economic development. The cult of Aphrodite also found expression in art. Sculptures and ceramic painting inspired by the goddess were widespread in Locri. Particularly noteworthy are the famous “pinakes” – terracotta votive tablets, often depicting scenes of Persephone’s abduction by Hades, which are a unique testament to local theology and aesthetics.

Syncretism and Identity

Locri, as a place where Greek traditions and local influences intermingled, offered a space for the formation of a unique cultural identity. The cult of Aphrodite served as a bridge between the Greek past and the local Italian context, binding diverse elements into a single identity that distinguished Locri from other cities of southern Italy. The cultivation of Aphrodite as a central figure in Locri’s pantheon strengthened the religious and social cohesion of the community and the city’s position as an important cultural centre in Magna Graecia.

The Archaeological Heritage of Locri – Testament to Ancient Greek Culture

Locri offers a rich archaeological heritage that constitutes a key source of information about ancient Greek colonies in Calabria. The archaeological area of Locri Epizefiri is described in different sources as covering more than 230 hectares or more than 300 hectares, but the accessible part of the park is about 150,000 m². Visitors therefore see only a fragment of the former polis, not the entire ancient city. The archaeological park extends across a large area, so it is best to allow 2 to 3 hours and avoid the midday heat in high summer.

Temple Ruins and Important Sacred Structures

Particularly noteworthy are the temples and sanctuaries found at Locri, which testify to the religious practices of the ancient inhabitants. The cult of Olympian Zeus is confirmed above all by the stone container from the sanctuary archive and the bronze tablets; the location of the temple itself remains debated. The Temple at Marasà (5th century BC), in the Ionic style, is striking for its monumentality. The quality and quantity of votive objects – items entrusted to the gods – reflect the high aesthetic and religious standards of the city.

Museum Finds and Their Cultural Significance

The terracotta decorative plaques known as pinakes contain mythological and religious scenes, bearing witness to the influence of Greece on Magna Graecian art. These plaques, alongside refined deity figurines, are precious examples of Greek art, while also showing Locri’s unique contribution to the development of architecture and artistic craftsmanship. Some of the most valuable collections, including the famous statues of the Dioscuri, are held in the National Archaeological Museum of Reggio Calabria. In the museum at Locri you can see material from the excavations, while the original bronze tablets from the archive of Olympian Zeus are in the Museo Archeologico Nazionale di Reggio Calabria; the tablets mainly concern the finances of the sanctuary and the polis.

Practical Visiting Information

As of March 2026, the site is open Wednesday to Sunday from 09:00 to 20:00, and closed on Monday and Tuesday. The standard ticket costs €5 full price and €2 reduced. The Museo archeologico nazionale and part of the park are open: Marasà and Parapezza. Casino Macrì and the Casa dei Leoni and Centocamere areas remain closed, so before travelling it is best to contact the museum and check the current scope of the visit. Sturdy footwear is essential – the terrain is extensive and uneven, with little shade in summer, so bring water and plan your visit for the morning or late afternoon.

Costa dei Gelsomini – a History Scented with Jasmine

Calabria’s Jasmine Heritage

The Locride region, in which Locri is situated, is more broadly known as the Costa dei Gelsomini – the Jasmine Coast. This poetic name is no accident: from the 1920s through to the 1970s, a stretch of coastline approximately 90 kilometres long was the world centre of jasmine cultivation (Jasminum grandiflorum). At its peak in the mid-1940s, Calabria supplied nearly 40% of the world’s demand for jasmine essence, which went directly to French perfume houses and became the base for some of the world’s most luxurious fragrances.

The Gelsominaie – Women Who Built a Legend

The history of the Jasmine Coast is above all a story of the gelsominaie – the jasmine pickers. Their work was extraordinarily demanding and became a symbol of the toil and determination of local women. Harvesting had to take place at night, typically from 3:00 in the morning, because the delicate flowers lost their precious aromas in sunlight. Young girls and women, often working barefoot and bent double, picked thousands of tiny flowers before dawn. These women became the pioneers of the struggle for workers’ rights in southern Italy, bringing about the signing of the first collective agreements in the sector in the late 1950s.

Modern Locri and the Region Today

Today the jasmine industry has given way to tourism and other agricultural production, such as bergamot and olive cultivation, but the memory of those times remains vivid. Modern Locri is a city of around 12,000 inhabitants that benefits in summer from music events in the town and nearby, including Ai Confini del Sud – World Music Festival and the Roccella Jazz Festival in nearby Roccella Ionica. Visitors are drawn by beaches recognised for the cleanliness of their waters: in 2026 the Blue Flag also includes Locri – Lungomare Zaleuco, alongside Siderno and Roccella Jonica. The beaches of Marina di Locri offer relaxed seaside leisure with views over the Ionian Sea and are a good complement to a visit to the archaeological park.

Getting Around – Locri as a Base and the Gem of Gerace

Gerace – Escape to Higher Ground

Just 10 kilometres from the coast rises the medieval town of Gerace, known as the “little Florence of Calabria”. It lies at an altitude of around 492 m above sea level, between the Novito and San Paolo fiumare, which helps explain its function as a naturally defended hilltop centre above the Locride. Its existence is inseparably linked to the decline of ancient Locri. When in the 7th–8th centuries AD the coastline became dangerous due to Saracen raids and spreading malaria, the inhabitants of Locri fled inland to a safe hill and founded Gerace. A visit to both places in a single day closes a historical loop – from ancient power by the sea to a medieval town on a high ridge. The most convenient way from Locri to Gerace is by taxi (around 15 minutes).

Transport and Recuperation

Locri is well connected with the rest of the region thanks to the railway line running along the coast. Trains from Reggio Calabria run an average of 13–16 times a day, and the journey takes 70 to 90 minutes, making Locri a realistic day-trip destination. For those seeking recuperation after sightseeing, the area offers the Terme di Antonimina (“Acque Sante Locresi”). These thermal springs, known since Roman times, offer sulphate waters at 36°C, helpful in treating rheumatic and dermatological conditions.

Locri – Frequently Asked Questions

What is Locri Epizefiri and why is it worth visiting?

Locri Epizefiri is an ancient Greek colony in Calabria, founded in the 7th century BC, known for the tradition of Zaleucus’s law code and the cult of Persephone. It is worth visiting for the combination of an extensive archaeological park with temple ruins and a theatre, a museum, and the beaches of the Ionian Sea.

How long should I allow for visiting the archaeological park at Locri?

For a thorough visit to the archaeological park and museum, allow 2 to 3 hours. The site is extensive and the most interesting areas, such as the Marasà temple and the theatre, are some distance apart. It is best to plan your visit for the morning or late afternoon, particularly in summer.

What are the beaches at Locri like – is it worth going just for the beach?

The beaches at Locri and across the Costa dei Gelsomini are sandy, wide and less crowded than in other parts of Italy. While the beach itself is attractive, the greatest value of Locri lies in the possibility of combining seaside relaxation with visits to the heritage of Magna Graecia.

How do I get to Locri by train from Reggio Calabria?

Getting there is very straightforward thanks to the Trenitalia railway line running along the Ionian coast. Trains from Reggio Calabria Centrale run many times a day from early morning to evening, with a journey time averaging 1 hour 10 minutes to 1.5 hours. A ticket costs around 10 euros.

Can Locri and Gerace be visited on the same day?

Yes, this is a very popular and logical combination. Gerace is just 10 km from Locri. The best plan is to visit the archaeological park at Locri in the morning, then take a taxi or bus to medieval Gerace for an afternoon stroll and dinner with views over the sunset.

What is the Costa dei Gelsomini?

The Costa dei Gelsomini, the Jasmine Coast, is the name for a stretch of coastline in the province of Reggio Calabria, including the area around Locri. The name derives from the historic jasmine cultivation that in the mid-20th century formed the basis of the local economy, supplying essence to the global perfume industry – including French fashion houses.

Main attractions

1

Locri Epizefiri Archaeological Park

Extensive ruins of a Greek colony from the 7th century BC. The Marasà temple, Sanctuary of Persephone, Greco-Roman theatre and necropolis. Combined ticket with the museum. Allow 2–3 hours; best visited in the morning.
2

National Museum of Locri

Collections from the excavations: copies/references to the bronze tablets from the archive of the sanctuary of Olympian Zeus and artefacts connected with the life of the polis; the code of Zaleucus is known from literary tradition, not from preserved tablets in the museum. There are also the famous pinakes, ceramics and coins. Part of the collection is complemented by the museum in Reggio Calabria.
3

Marina di Locri Beaches

Sandy, wide beaches on the Costa dei Gelsomini with clean Ionian Sea water. A good place to combine history with seaside relaxation, especially after a morning visit to the archaeological park.
4

Gerace – 10 km from Locri

A medieval hill town founded by refugees from ancient Locri. Calabria’s largest cathedral, consecrated in 1045; Gerace is historically known as the “city of a hundred churches”, although around thirty survive today.
5

Capo Bruzzano – Traditional Coastal Point

Capo Bruzzano is sometimes linked with the tradition of the colonists’ first landing, but it is not a confirmed underwater port of Locri. The location of the ancient port remains uncertain and may have taken the form of a simple coastal landing place or a harbour at the mouth of a fiumara.
6

Terme di Antonimina

Thermal springs known since Roman times – sulphate waters at 36°C, indicated for rheumatic conditions. The ideal complement to an active day of sightseeing. Located 15 km from Locri.

Getting there

By plane

From Poland to Lamezia Terme (SUF) – seasonal direct Ryanair flights; outside the season via Rome or Milan. Reggio Calabria airport (REG, 90 km) is closer – direct flights from Katowice (Ryanair) and from Rome and Milan. Lamezia is 130 km farther away.

By train

From Reggio Calabria: 13–16 services daily, about 70–90 min, ticket about €10. Locri station is in the centre; the archaeological park in contrada Marasà is about 3.5–5 km away, so a bus, taxi or car is more convenient.

By car

The most convenient option. From Reggio airport via the SS106 along the Ionian coast, about 90 km (1h 15 min). Parking at the museum and on the beach.

By bus

Regional buses along the Ionian coast connect Locri with Reggio Calabria, Siderno and Caulonia. A local bus runs to Gerace (10 km).
