The Sanctuary of Santa Maria dell'Isola is the best-known symbol of Tropea and one of Calabria's most recognisable landmarks. The church crowns a 33-metre sandstone rock that was once a separate island – over the centuries, marine sediment joined it to the mainland, but the name endured. The sight of its white silhouette against the turquoise Tyrrhenian Sea is one of the most characteristic views of not just Tropea, but of Calabria as a whole.

A Thousand Years of Monastic History

The sanctuary's origins probably reach back to the early Middle Ages, when ascetic monks of the Byzantine tradition settled on the rock; later, the Isola also hosted a community of Basilian monks. They arrived in Calabria in large numbers during the 8th–9th centuries, partly in connection with iconoclasm and instability in the Byzantine world. For several centuries, the cliff served as a hermitage, and a local religious tradition took shape around it. In the 11th century, during the Norman period, the hermitage was placed under the care of the Benedictines.

The sanctuary's official history states that in the 11th century the Isola passed to the Benedictines of Montecassino. The donation is attributed to Princess Sichelgaita and was confirmed in 1090 by an act of her son, Duke Roger Borsa. The connection with the Abbey of Montecassino remains an important part of the site's history. Today's building, however, is not an early medieval original but the result of many reconstructions after earthquakes.

Basilian monks were religious of the Byzantine tradition, living according to the Rule of St Basil. Their presence in Calabria was particularly strong between the 8th and 11th centuries and left behind a legacy of Greek liturgy, eremitic tradition and a network of monasteries linked to the Byzantine heritage of southern Italy. This is key to understanding a region where Greek, Byzantine and Latin influences intermingled for centuries.

The site's history was far from peaceful, however. The earthquakes of 1783 and 1905 badly damaged the building, and the sanctuary's present appearance is largely the result of later reconstructions. Despite these changes, the place has retained its austere, contemplative character – the interior is dominated by simplicity and an atmosphere of seclusion that suits its position on a rock above the sea.

Around the feast of 15 August, the Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary, religious celebrations take place in connection with the sanctuary and Tropea's seaside character. The 18th-century statue of the Holy Family kept in the sanctuary is then carried out of the church and taken out to sea in procession on a decorated fishing boat. The programme of events may change, so it is best to check current information locally before your visit.

Rock-Carved Steps, Gardens and Panoramas

The path to the summit leads up stone steps carved into the rock. The climb can be tiring, especially in the heat – rest spots appear along the way, and with each level the view becomes ever wider. Comfortable shoes and a bottle of water are recommended, especially in summer, when the sandstone absorbs the heat of the sun.

Behind the church stretch gardens filled with Mediterranean vegetation – surprisingly expansive for a cliff-top setting. Pathways wind among oleanders, agaves, prickly pears, and olive trees. It is a peaceful spot with a wide view of the Tyrrhenian Sea and, in good visibility, of Stromboli and the silhouettes of the other Aeolian Islands. Within the sanctuary complex there is also a small museum with artefacts found on the site, including medieval tombs and valuable statues.

The best-known view of Santa Maria dell'Isola is from the viewpoint at Corso Vittorio Emanuele / Piazza del Cannone, where you can see the rock, the beach and the Tyrrhenian Sea in one frame. A different perspective comes from the beach at the foot of the rock – especially in the morning and at golden hour. Beneath the sanctuary lies the Grotta del Palombaro – a sea cave with a tiny beach, most easily reached from the water by kayak, pedalo or boat. If you want photos without crowds, it is best to come early, as in high season the area fills up quickly.

From Tropea's beaches below, you can admire the sanctuary from below – a perspective that best shows the height of the rock and its striking position. The descent to the beach leads down steep steps from the town, so factor that into your walk.

Practical Tips

Opening hours: According to the sanctuary's official website, it is open in April, May and October from 9:30–19:30 and from June to September from 9:30–20:00; in January, February, March, November and December it is closed. Check for updates before visiting. Entry times may also depend on religious services and the current organisation of visitor access.

How to get there: From Tropea's old town, you can reach the vicinity of the sanctuary in about 10–15 minutes, and then there is the climb up the rock steps. The steps to the top rule out pushchairs and are a barrier for anyone with limited mobility. Allow about 30–60 minutes for the visit. In summer, the approach is often in full sun, so it is best to go in the morning or late afternoon and bring water and a hat.

Tickets: According to current information from the local portal Tropea.biz, entry to the garden, the historical-liturgical route and the panoramic terrace costs €3.00 for visitors over 18, €2.00 for children and teenagers aged 7–18, and is free for children up to 6 years old. Prices and visiting rules may change, so check them on site.

Public transport: Tropea lies on the coastal branch of the Tyrrhenian line (the via Tropea route, Lamezia Terme – Rosarno). It is served mainly by regional trains; the only long-distance services stopping here are a few overnight Intercity Notte trains from Turin and Milan – Frecciarossa and daytime Intercity trains run inland via Vibo Valentia-Pizzo station. From the station to the historic centre is about 10–15 minutes on foot, and from there another 10–15 minutes to the sanctuary area. The nearest airport is Lamezia Terme (SUF), about 55–60 km from Tropea.

Best time to visit: Early morning or late afternoon. In July and August, midday can be very tiring, as the rock offers little shade. If you hope to see Stromboli, remember it is only visible in good visibility – best in the morning, after rain or in low humidity. The volcano is about 60 km from Tropea as the crow flies.

After the sanctuary, head on to Capo Vaticano, about 20–30 minutes away by car, or explore the rest of Tropea.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there an entrance fee for Santa Maria dell'Isola?

The church itself is sometimes accessible without an additional fee, while according to current information from the local portal Tropea.biz, entry to the garden, the historical-liturgical route and the panoramic terrace costs €3.00 for visitors over 18, €2.00 for children and teenagers aged 7–18, and is free for children up to 6 years old. Check the current rules before entering.

How long does a visit take?

Usually about 30–60 minutes is enough for the ascent, a short walk through the gardens and a stop at the viewpoints.

Are the steps difficult for elderly visitors?

The steps are steep and can be demanding, especially in the heat. There is no access adapted for pushchairs or a comfortable alternative without steps, so it is best to take it at a leisurely pace.

When does the maritime procession take place?

The maritime procession is connected with the celebrations of the Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary in mid-August. In 2026, the celebrations began on 14 August with a concert by the Città di Tropea Wind Orchestra and a polyphonic choir, and on 15 August the maritime procession and an evening fireworks display took place. The 18th-century statue of the Holy Family kept in the sanctuary is taken out to sea on a decorated fishing boat.
